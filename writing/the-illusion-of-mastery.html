The illusion of mastery: A brain dump on the Dunning-Kruger effect.

What if your strongest areas of confidence are actually your biggest blind spots?

13 Jul 2026 · Also on Substack

Ever heard a diabolical take from someone very confident in their speech but they are totally wrong on the topic? Well two researchers from Cornell University in 1999 set out to answer this question. Two psychologists, David Dunning and Justin Kruger who were inspired by a very unlikely case of a man named McArthur Wheeler, who robbed two Pittsburgh banks in broad daylight with no disguise. Wheeler believed that rubbing lemon juice on his face would make him invisible to security cameras. They set out to understand self-assessment where their research found out people who scored lower in tests actually ranked themselves higher when asked to predetermine how well they did in the test. Funny right??

The Dunning-Kruger effect is a cognitive bias where novices feel overconfident about their competence on a topic. A novice/beginner/noob is just someone who is less competent on a subject.

Dunning-Kruger Effect occurs when people with limited knowledge or skill in a specific area significantly overestimate their competence because the same abilities needed for good performance are required to recognize good performance, creating a dual burden of incompetence and poor self-awareness.

Very few people are competent enough to understand the level of details in their own field of interest. This means that most people don’t know what they do not know. Inflated ego and overconfidence are also markers of “stupidity.” People who have these traits tend to be seen as leaders because their ego and overconfidence makes them attractive. It is a bias that the community has and usually rewards.

When you are a noob, you don’t know what you don’t know. This is because the same skill required to produce a correct output is needed to recognize what a correct output looks like. In tech, this is simply a “Skill Issue”.

Most noobs sit at the peak of Mount Stupid where they are overconfident on an illusion of mastery. As they learn more their confidence crashes into the valley of despair. From there they learn to align self-assessment with reality, hence their confidence and competence climb gradually towards genuine expertise. This is not about intelligence levels but a gap between perceived competence vs. actual, a blind spot created by inexperience or being a noob at a specific topic.

Why do incompetent people rarely know they’re incompetent?

  1. Dual burden of incompetence: a novice chess player can’t see why their strategy is flawed because they don’t know yet how to think strategically.
  2. Self-assessment (metacognition) requires reference points that a beginner lacks as they lack the expertise to step back and evaluate their thinking.
  3. Fear of destroying the self-image of competence; not wanting to sound stupid because our brains have built fortresses around the ideas that we have won in the past. So we have confirmation bias which compounds this problem as we seek even more evidence to prove we are competent enough.

A good example of this would be a lucky investor who just invested at the right time decides to give financial advice on Instagram not knowing they are just lucky and skill was not an attribute to their success. I am sorry if this feels offensive.

Then comes the expertise paradox. If you have been long enough in tech you have heard the classic senior engineer answer “Well, it depends”, in hindsight this may look like imposter syndrome where the engineer sees a lot of pitfalls in a solution they might provide different from the beginner who just sees a simple straight-forward solution. But this is actually a caveat of being very competent, you see things in a whole new landscape of tradeoffs.

Culture often plays a major role here, research has found that in cultures where self-assessment and humility is emphasized the effect seemed to be moderate e.g. in East Asia.

How do we overcome this?

I won’t sit here at my keyboard and lie this does not get to me. It does but one of the ways I am learning daily is to embrace intellectual humility. This means seeking out evidence that contradicts my current belief sets on specific fields I am interested in. Another one would be assessing myself by what the experts in that field actually do and I end up seeing so many gaps. Reading advanced material for example in Math such as information theory taught me how little I know in Linear Algebra among many other math fields where I thought I was good enough. If you feel confident enough try teaching it to someone else or writing about it, well you might find out you actually lack depth. Asking more experienced colleagues also what you miss in depth or knowledge also helps cement this approach in discussions.

The verdict: Being confident does not mean you are competent. Our biggest blind spots sit where we feel most certain.
